## Supported claims and corrections

**Preference and off-axis:** Toole 1986 Part II abstract limits the reported relationship to the studied listeners and conventional forward-facing speaker designs. It combines multiple free-field orientations with subjective ratings, and §6.2/Fig. 11 reports fewer small irregularities in DI and sound power for the better-rated groups. The same section notes similarity of the studied speaker sizes/configurations. Section 13 warns that on-axis interference can cause apparently erratic DI and cautions against using DI as a general figure of merit. This supports scrutinizing spectral and spatial behavior; it does not establish a prescribed DI trajectory, perfect constant-directivity as a universal preference, or horn-only preference rankings.

**Olive model:** Patent Eq. 9 reproduces the coefficients in the repository model: 12.69, -2.49 for NBD_ON, -2.99 for NBD_PIR, -4.31 for LFX and +2.32 for SM_PIR. Patent Eq. 5 defines SM as the regression goodness-of-fit for a response curve versus log frequency over 100 Hz–16 kHz; it is **not DI smoothness**. In the larger-data example, the patent reports initial 13-speaker fit degrading when tested against 57 more, then a generalized model developed using the combined 70. It is observational and depends on the model, variables, measurement and tested systems. Greater smoothness of a predicted response is not the same as proving that increasing DI improves preference; overall response and slope, room and direct sound also matter. The regression equation is confirmed in the patent, not newly verified from the paywalled Part II paper.

**PIR composition:** Toole's p. 17 gives a room-response estimate using 12% listening-window, 44% early-reflection and 44% sound-power components for typical furnished domestic rooms, and explicitly allows other room proportions. Those are components of an acoustic prediction, not listener preference coefficients. The listening window is itself an angular average, not a single on-axis line. The 44+44=88% arithmetic cannot establish a 3:1 off-axis perceptual weighting, nor that v7's on-axis terms account for only 12% of preference. The CTA public listing does not establish this particular mixture as a normative requirement; do not attribute it to the standard without the applicable text.

**CD versus rising DI:** Sridhar et al. §2 defines strict pattern invariance over a frequency range and a weaker DI-invariance criterion; §3.4 defines DI standard deviation as a CD metric. A small scalar variation cannot guarantee unchanged complete polar shape. It is mathematically immediate that a monotonic nonzero slope has nonzero standard deviation even if perfectly smooth; e.g., values 0,1,2 dB have standard deviation sqrt(2/3) dB. Thus the scoring dossier's repeated claim that `distd` generally penalizes jaggedness while accepting a smooth rise is incorrect. Removing a fitted trend before measuring residual variation is a conceptually distinct calculation whose behavior, weighting, units and noise sensitivity would need specification and evidence; no change is proposed here.

**No sourced 1–3 dB/oct preference law:** No primary source inspected establishes this as a universal optimum, a 4–5 dB/oct rejection threshold, a fair generic horn score, or a required change from a zero target. A zero DI slope is a design objective for DI constancy, not a mathematical bug or necessarily a preference optimum. A smooth nonzero trajectory can be evaluated, but whether it is desirable depends on whole-system direct sound, radiation, listening area, room, use, crossover and preference. Do not replace one unsupported universal target with another. `dB/decade = dB/octave / log10(2)` and `dB/octave = dB/decade * log10(2)`; never equate the values.

**System/crossover:** A horn-plus-source-alone score cannot determine complete-system sound power, listening-window/PIR, listener preference, or the acoustic result of a crossover. The woofer and horn contributions combine as complex pressures with relative phase, acoustic centers, filter transfer functions, position and enclosure; matched scalar DI values at one frequency do not guarantee matched polar shape or smooth summed response. Sheerin's author comparison presents DI handover as a useful diagnostic for specific horn and woofer pairings; it is not proof that 4–7 dB or any fixed value is a universal woofer target, nor that mismatch necessarily creates the exact on-axis lobe claimed in the scoring dossier. Toole Part II §13 also flags off-axis performance as important to crossover assessment. Assess the actual overlap and angles if system information becomes available.

## The two cited slope estimates

- **Sheerin 2.6 dB/oct:** the author says the *modeled* Le Cleac'h trace rises roughly 4 dB at 1.4 kHz to 12 dB at 12 kHz; endpoint arithmetic gives `8/log2(12/1.4) = 2.58 dB/oct`, or about 8.57 dB/dec. This is an endpoint secant over that particular band, not a regression fit, experimental measurement of every Le Cleac'h horn, or listening-preference optimum. The source describes a fully coupled FEA model; it says its modeling has been validated against experiments generally, but does not supply a matched measured DI trace and fit for this example. `HORN_WHITEPAPERS` table's heading `T~0.8` cannot be inferred from this case without independently verified source parameters.
- **Crowe 4.1 dB/oct:** `(16-2)/log2(16/1.5) = 4.10 dB/oct`, 13.62 dB/dec, is correct arithmetic **conditional on those endpoint values**. The cited Crowe HF1460 report states 1 m frequency responses at 0°, 15°, 30°, 45° and a 10 cm near-field reading; 6/12 dB/oct are its **frequency-response** falloffs, not DI slopes. No full-sphere DI series or 2→16 dB endpoint values were located in its text. Four responses on one orbit cannot establish complete spherical DI. The repository's source of those endpoints, ES-600 vs ES-450 identity, normalisation, band, angular integration and fit remain unresolved; do not represent 4.1 as measured Crowe DI.
