1. Introduction
        The design of high output, wide-bandwidth loudspeaker systems with controlled
directivity presents a set of competing constraints in acoustic, geometric, and ergonomic
domains. Conventional multiway direct radiating systems are often limited by spatial separation
between sources, which leads to frequency dependent interference and off-axis lobing through
the crossover regions. While careful crossover design and driver placement can mitigate these
effects, they cannot eliminate the underlying issue of distributed acoustic origins.
        Coaxial topologies address this limitation by approximating a coincident source,
improving phase coherence and polar consistency. However, such systems introduce their own
constraints, including limitations in scalability, amplitude modulation distortion, and achievable
bandwidth partitioning across transducers. In high-output applications, these constraints can
become prohibitive.
        Multiple entry horn (MEH) and related waveguide architectures offer an alternative
approach by acoustically summing multiple sources into a shared radiating aperture. When
properly implemented, these systems can approximate a point source while maintaining high
efficiency and controlled directivity. However, practical implementations are constrained by
geometric requirements - particularly the need to maintain sufficiently small source-to-source
spacing relative to the wavelengths at crossover frequencies, while simultaneously preserving
favorable waveguide expansion near the throat.
        This document introduces the Shared Aperture Waveguide Module (SAWMOD), a
compact three-way loudspeaker topology that integrates high frequency and midrange
transducers into a unified waveguide structure with an augmented low frequency section
featuring a passive side/rear rejection mechanism. The design incorporates a unique waveguide
geometry featuring localized contour elements (“knuckles”) that enable reduced effective source
spacing for bandpass-coupled midrange drivers while maintaining controlled wavefront
expansion.
        ​
​       The presented mechanisms of this paper are:
             ●​ A waveguide topology that relaxes conventional geometric constraints associated
                with multiple entry horn designs
             ●​ A method for improving acoustic source spacing without excessive disruption to
                throat expansion
             ●​ A parametric contouring approach that enables targeted directivity control
                through wavefront shaping
             ●​ An integrated low-frequency system that extends pattern control to lower
                frequencies through spatial and acoustic interaction mechanisms

       The following sections describe the design objectives, geometric formulation, and
acoustic behavior of the SAWMOD system, supported by simulation and measurement based
analysis and experimental observations.

2. Acoustic Objectives
The system is designed as a three way architecture with defined crossover regions chosen to
balance bandwidth allocation, directivity control, and transducer performance:

   ●​ Low-Mid Crossover: ~350 Hz
   ●​ Mid-High Crossover: ~1.6 kHz

A key objective is to maintain consistent directivity through these crossover regions. In
particular, the mid-high transition imposes a stringent constraint on allowable spatial separation
between acoustic sources. As a design guideline, the effective center-to-center spacing
between radiating elements should remain on the order of ≤¼ wavelength at the crossover
frequency to minimize off-axis interference. For a 1.6 kHz crossover, this corresponds to
approximately 53.5 mm.

The target nominal coverage pattern of the system is:

   ●​ Horizontal: 100°
   ●​ Vertical: 70°

Maintaining this coverage with minimal frequency-dependent narrowing or lobing is a primary
design goal. Achieving the desired acoustic performance within a compact form factor
introduces several geometric challenges:

2.1 Waveguide Throat Region
   ●​ The waveguide must support smooth wavefront expansion from the high frequency
      compression driver while accommodating the injection of midrange energy. Conventional
      approaches often require placing midrange injection ports close to the throat to satisfy
      spacing constraints, which can disrupt the initial expansion and introduce acoustic
      discontinuities.

2.2 Midrange Source Placement
Bandpass-loaded midrange drivers must be positioned such that:
   ●​ Their effective acoustic centers remain within the spacing constraint at the crossover
      frequency
   ●​ Their coupling into the waveguide minimizes high-Q resonances and excessive
      diffraction

2.3 Aperture Development
   ●​ The waveguide must transition from a compact throat to a rectangular mouth geometry
      suitable for pattern control and arrayability (for future designs sharing this topology),
      while preserving a controlled directivity profile across the operating bandwidth.

The integration of low-frequency transducers introduces additional requirements:

   ●​ Extension of directivity control below the midrange band
   ●​ Efficient use of enclosure volume within a compact footprint
   ●​ Thermal and mechanical considerations for high power operation

The selected configuration utilizes dual sidefiring low frequency drivers coupled to a shared
enclosure volume, with spatial arrangement chosen to influence the system’s polar response
near the low-mid crossover region.


3. Waveguide Topology
       For the purpose of this document, this waveguide topology is referred to as a three
segment device consisting of axial cross section segments denoted as S1 (the throat), S2, (the
segment located at the centroid of the bandpass injection ports), and S3 (the segment at the
mouth).




The SAWMOD waveguide incorporates localized inward-extending contour features at the S2
region, referred to as knuckles. These features modify the cross sectional geometry of the
waveguide in a manner that is not captured by traditional monotonic flare descriptions. Rather
than uniformly expanding from throat to mouth, the waveguide introduces controlled local
deviations that alter both the spatial distribution of the boundary and the effective propagation
path of the wavefront. The knuckles serve two primary geometric functions:

4.3 Simulation Results
Performance between the two variants is largely similar, with a few notable differences:
   1.​ The additional degree of freedom afforded by the knuckle features allows for a
       compensatory pattern widening from 3-7kHz. The comparison between simulated and
       measured indicates that the pattern was successfully optimized for constant directivity.
   2.​ Radiation impedance has increased slightly.
   3.​ The wavefront shape at the mouth is largely similar but with a slightly more
       rectangular-bounded pattern in the HF.

        The SAWMOD waveguide departs from conventional monotonic flare geometries by
introducing controlled, localized contour features that serve both geometric and acoustic
functions. By doing so, it addresses a central limitation of multiple entry horn designs: the
tradeoff between source spacing and waveguide expansion integrity.
        The resulting topology enables closer effective spacing of midrange injection sources,
controlled wavefront evolution through critical regions of the device and additional flexibility in
shaping directivity without increasing overall system size

The SAWMOD features two sidefiring 10
inch low frequency transducers that share
a 22 liter sealed chamber and are
mounted magnet-out. This allows for
superior thermal performance compared
to typical sealed chamber low frequency
transducers as air exchange with the
motor structures can happen freely.
Additionally, mounting the transducers
reversed allows for a more compact
arrangement as the central waveguide
module occupies a significant portion of
the sealed chamber. The center-center
spacing of each 10” transducer is
optimized to create a directional interference pattern to aid side rejection, thus lowering the
horizontal directivity control cutoff. The system is acoustically crossed to the midrange section at
approximately 350 Hz, a frequency chosen to balance wavelength-relative source spacing with
practical enclosure constraints.

5.1 Slot-Loaded Radiation Behavior
        Each low-frequency driver radiates through a semi-enclosed side slot formed by the
enclosure geometry. This slot-loaded configuration modifies the radiation impedance and
effective loading conditions experienced by the transducers relative to a conventional sealed
enclosure.

       Simulation results comparing the slot-loaded configuration to a conventional sealed
arrangement indicate a measurable increase in on-axis sensitivity in the upper bass and
low–midrange region.




        While the slot structure introduces resonant behavior characteristic of a Helmholtz
system, its influence on system response is best understood as part of a coupled acoustic
system rather than a simple resonator in isolation. At frequencies approaching the low–mid
crossover, the spatial separation between the two low frequency drivers becomes a dominant
factor in determining the horizontal radiation pattern.
        Simulation of the two sources in isolation (i.e., without the slot structure) shows the
expected interference pattern based on their center-to-center spacing, including the formation of
lateral and rearward lobes.




These results establish a baseline expectation for the achievable degree of side and rear
rejection based purely on geometric spacing. In this configuration, the level of off-axis

attenuation is limited by the relatively large wavelengths involved and the finite spacing between
sources.

5.2 Measured Slot Behavior
Measurements of an early SAWMOD prototype reveal a significantly different radiation pattern
than predicted by spacing alone. In particular, a pronounced off-axis cancellation is observed,
accompanied by increased rear rejection relative to the simulated baseline.




This cancellation occurs at frequencies corresponding to the resonant behavior of the side-slot
structure and is only present when both the low-frequency and midrange sections are active.
When measured independently, neither the low-frequency nor midrange sections exhibit this null
to the same degree. The below measurements were taken at 90 degrees off-axis illustrating the
null that occurs only when both sets of drivers are playing concurrently.




The measured behavior suggests the presence of a coupled acoustic interaction between:

   ●​ The low-frequency transducers
   ●​ The midrange bandpass system
   ●​ The side-slot cavity structure

A plausible interpretation is that the slot volume and its associated resonance introduce a
frequency-dependent phase offset between the radiated fields of the low frequency and



                                                                                               12
midrange sources. At specific angles and frequencies, this phase relationship results in
enhanced destructive interference, producing the observed off-axis null.

However, this mechanism has not been fully characterized, and the interaction should be
considered an observed system-level phenomenon rather than a fully modeled or optimized
feature at this stage of development.

To better understand and control this behavior, a series of physical modifications were made to
the side-slot structure by introducing openings (“holes”) in the slot boundaries. These
modifications alter the acoustic impedance and damping characteristics of the cavity, thereby
affecting its interaction with the radiated field.




This demonstrates that the slot structure plays an active role in shaping the system’s polar
response and can be tuned to achieve different trade-offs between forward directivity, side
rejection, and pattern smoothness.

5.3 Low Frequency Integration Summary
The low frequency section of the SAWMOD system combines spatial source arrangement and
enclosure-based acoustic interaction to extend directional control below the midrange band.

Key observations include:

   ●​ The slot-loaded configuration increases acoustic efficiency in the 150-400 Hz region
      relative to a conventional sealed enclosure
   ●​ Source spacing alone provides a baseline level of directional control, but does not fully
      account for measured behavior
   ●​ Additional off-axis cancellation effects arise from coupled interactions between the low
      frequency drivers, midrange system, and slot cavity
   ●​ The magnitude and character of these effects can be modified through changes to the
      slot geometry

These results indicate that the low-frequency system operates as an integrated acoustic
structure rather than a set of independent radiators, and that enclosure geometry can play a
significant role in shaping system-level directivity. Further investigation is required to fully
characterize and predict these interactions.


6. Conclusion
        The Shared Aperture Waveguide Module (SAWMOD) is a compact multiway
loudspeaker topology intended to address the limitations of conventional direct radiating,
coaxial, and multiple entry horn systems. The approach centers on integrating multiple acoustic
sources into a shared waveguide structure while maintaining controlled directivity and
minimizing interference through critical crossover regions.
        A key feature of the design is the introduction of localized contour modifications
(“knuckles”) within the waveguide, enabling reduced effective source spacing for
bandpass-coupled midrange drivers without compromising near-throat wavefront development.
Simulation results indicate that this approach supports more consistent directivity behavior
through the mid-high crossover region, while providing additional flexibility in shaping the polar
response through geometric control.
        The low frequency system extends this shared aperture concept by combining spatial
source arrangement with enclosure-based acoustic interaction. Simulated and measured results
show that the slot-loaded configuration increases efficiency in the upper bass and low midrange
region, while also contributing to directional control. Measurements further indicate the presence
of coupled interactions between the low frequency, midrange, and enclosure structures,
producing off-axis cancellation effects not predicted by source spacing alone. These behaviors
highlight the role of enclosure geometry as an active element in system-level directivity, though
their underlying mechanisms require further investigation.
